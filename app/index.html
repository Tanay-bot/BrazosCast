The measure

A stage is a height. Your street is the answer.

The gauge reports feet above its own zero, not a verdict. 20 ft is action stage — the river is out of its banks somewhere. 45 ft is flood stage. Neither is about you.

What matters is the stage at which water reaches the low point of your street, and the hours between the crest passing upstream and that moment. BrazosCast answers that per street segment, from the county's own address ranges, the national HAND flood model and the observed 2017 flood — and it shows the error on every number it prints.

For households

Know which of your streets goes first.

Add the addresses you actually care about — home, a parent's house, the school — and they are ranked by when each is reached, not by how high the river is. Save them to an account and they are there on any phone. The answer keeps working when the storm takes the connection, because it was computed before the storm.

For responders

Which roads are cut while the street is still dry.

The inverse question is the operational one: a street with no water on it and no dry route to a hospital. The app computes that from the road graph at the stage you choose, alongside the routes that still reach a fire station — and it says when the answer is a boundary of the study area rather than a real cut.

For planning and press

Every number carries the error it was measured at.

Thresholds scored against the observed 2017 extent, then against a second flood the thresholds were not fitted on. The app publishes the result even where it is unflattering: its own thresholds are biased low, and it says so on the answer itself rather than in a footnote.

The limits

What this does not claim.

  • Road depths are modelled, never observed. A threshold is a model output.
  • Flood extents are provisional pending a unit check on the HAND raster.
  • A refuge is ground the modelled water does not reach at 50 ft — not a shelter.
  • Responder destinations are volunteer-mapped OpenStreetMap points, not a dispatch feed.
  • Twelve inches of moving water carries most cars away. Never drive through water.
